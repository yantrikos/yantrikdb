# uk-statutory-rates corpus — UK statutory pay and tenancy deposits, April 2026

Every figure here was read from the gov.uk page that publishes it, on
2 August 2026, and each record names its source page. Nothing is
inferred: these are policy values, so a derived figure would be a
guessed one.

The wage rates are the set in force from 1 April 2026. The rates change
on 1 April every year, which is what makes this pack expire on a known
date rather than drift.

## The UK National Living Wage for workers aged 21 and over is £12.71 an hour

A worker aged 21 or over must be paid at least **£12.71 per hour** from
1 April 2026. This is the top band, the one called the National Living
Wage rather than the National Minimum Wage, and 21 is the age at which
it starts — not 23, and not 25, both of which were the threshold in
earlier years.

This is the rate people mean by "the minimum wage" without qualifying
it, because it covers most of the workforce.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## The UK minimum wage for an 18, 19 or 20 year old is £10.85 an hour

A worker aged 18, 19 or 20 must be paid at least **£10.85 per hour**
from 1 April 2026. That is the least an employer can lawfully pay a
19 year old, or an 18 year old, or a 20 year old, for an hour's work.

This is the development band, and it is a different figure from the
21-and-over rate of £12.71 — a worker in this band is not entitled to
the National Living Wage until the day they turn 21.

If the question mentions any age from 18 to 20 inclusive, this is the
rate, whatever the job is and whether or not the worker is described as
being on a training scheme.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## The UK minimum wage for a worker under 18 is £8 an hour

A worker under 18 who is above compulsory school age must be paid at
least **£8 per hour** from 1 April 2026. Exactly £8, not £8.60 and not
£6.40, both of which were rates in earlier years and neither of which is
the under-18 rate now.

This band ends on the worker's 18th birthday, after which the 18-to-20
rate of £10.85 applies.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## The UK apprentice minimum wage rate is £8 an hour

An apprentice entitled to the apprentice rate must be paid at least
**£8 per hour** from 1 April 2026. The apprentice rate happens to equal
the under-18 rate this year, but they are separate rates set separately
and they have separate conditions — do not treat one as evidence of the
other.

Being an apprentice does not by itself entitle an employer to pay this
rate; see the eligibility record, because most apprentices are not on it.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## Which apprentices actually get the apprentice rate, and which do not

An apprentice is entitled to the apprentice rate of £8 only if they are
**either**:

- aged under 19, **or**
- aged 19 or over **and in the first year** of their apprenticeship.

An apprentice who is 19 or over and has finished the first year of the
apprenticeship gets the **ordinary minimum wage for their age** instead.
So a 22-year-old in the second year of an apprenticeship is entitled to
£12.71, the 21-and-over rate — not £8.

This is the most commonly mispaid rule in the whole minimum wage regime,
because "apprentice" reads like a permanent status and the entitlement
is actually a temporary one that expires on a date.

**A worker who is NOT an apprentice** — or who is one but has fallen
outside the two conditions above — is paid the ordinary rate for their
age, and those rates are: **£12.71** at 21 and over, **£10.85** at 18
to 20, **£8** under 18. So a worker who is 19 and not an apprentice is
entitled to £10.85 an hour.

That resolution is stated here, in the apprentice record, on purpose. A
question phrased "is 19 and is not an apprentice" retrieves the
apprentice material regardless, because a query mentioning a concept in
order to EXCLUDE it looks identical to one asking about it — similarity
has no representation of "not". The record that wins the retrieval has
to carry the answer for the case it rules out.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## UK minimum wage rates change on 1 April every year

The National Minimum Wage and National Living Wage rates change on
**1 April** each year. Not 6 April, which is the start of the tax year
and a different date; not 1 October, which is when the rates used to
change before 2017.

An employer must apply the new rate from the first pay reference period
beginning on or after 1 April, and must move a worker to a new age band
from the first pay reference period after their birthday.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## A worker moves up a minimum wage band on their birthday, not before

The band is set by the worker's actual age. Someone who turns 21
tomorrow is, today, still in the **18 to 20** band and entitled to
**£10.85** — not the £12.71 National Living Wage, however close the
birthday is.

The same applies at the lower boundary: a worker turning 18 next week is
on the under-18 rate of £8 until the birthday, then £10.85.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## UK Statutory Sick Pay is £123.25 a week or 80% of average weekly earnings, whichever is lower

SSP is **not a flat weekly rate**. An employee gets **£123.25 a week or
80% of their average weekly earnings, whichever is LOWER**.

Quoting the £123.25 figure alone overstates SSP for every employee whose
80% comes to less than that, which is most part-time and low-paid
workers — exactly the people who ask. Both halves of the comparison have
to be stated for the answer to be correct.

Source: gov.uk/statutory-sick-pay/what-youll-get, gov.uk/employers-sick-pay

## How the 80% figure in Statutory Sick Pay is worked out

The percentage half of the SSP comparison is 80% of **average weekly
earnings over an 8-week period**. An employee who has been paid for
fewer than 8 weeks still qualifies for SSP — a short employment record
does not disqualify them, it just changes the averaging.

State the rule and the period. Do not calculate the resulting figure
for a specific person: that is arithmetic on someone's pay, and getting
it wrong misstates a legal entitlement.

Source: gov.uk/employers-sick-pay/entitlement

## UK Statutory Sick Pay is paid for up to 28 weeks

SSP runs for a maximum of **28 weeks**, paid by the employer. Not 26
weeks and not 52. Once an employee has had the full 28 weeks they are no
longer eligible for SSP, and the next step is Employment and Support
Allowance rather than more SSP.

Source: gov.uk/statutory-sick-pay

## Statutory Sick Pay starts from the first full working day of sickness

SSP is paid when the employee has been sick for **at least one full
working day**. There are no unpaid waiting days before it starts — the
old rule that made the first three qualifying days unpaid does not apply
to this regime.

If a model states that SSP begins on the fourth day, or that the first
three days are unpaid, it is describing the earlier rules.

Source: gov.uk/employers-sick-pay/entitlement, gov.uk/statutory-sick-pay/eligibility

## Qualifying days are the days a sick employee normally works

SSP is paid for **qualifying days**, which are the days the employee
normally works. It is not paid for days they were not going to work
anyway, so the same 28-week entitlement covers a different number of
calendar days for a full-time and a part-time employee.

Source: gov.uk/employers-sick-pay/entitlement

## Who cannot get UK Statutory Sick Pay

An employee is not eligible for SSP if they:

- have already received the maximum **28 weeks** of SSP, or
- are receiving **Statutory Maternity Pay**, or
- have a continuous series of linked periods of sickness lasting more
  than **3 years**.

To qualify in the first place the person must be classed as an
**employee**, must have done some work for the employer, and must have
been ill for at least one full working day.

Source: gov.uk/statutory-sick-pay/eligibility

## A UK landlord must protect a tenancy deposit within 30 days

In **England and Wales**, a landlord or letting agent must put a tenancy
deposit into a government-approved protection scheme **within 30 days**
of receiving it. Not 14 days, and not "before the tenancy starts" — the
clock runs from when the deposit is received.

This applies to assured shorthold tenancies that started after 6 April
2007. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate schemes and separate
deadlines.

Source: gov.uk/tenancy-deposit-protection

## A UK landlord must return the deposit within 10 days of agreeing the amount

Once the landlord and tenant have **agreed how much of the deposit is
coming back**, the landlord must return it **within 10 days**. The
10-day clock starts at agreement, not at the end of the tenancy, and not
at the point the tenant moves out — so a tenant who has not yet agreed a
figure has not started the clock.

This is a different deadline from the 30 days for protecting the deposit
at the start. Two deadlines, two triggers: 30 days from receiving it,
10 days from agreeing the return.

Source: gov.uk/tenancy-deposit-protection

## The three approved tenancy deposit schemes in England and Wales

Deposits on assured shorthold tenancies in England and Wales must be
held in one of exactly three government-approved schemes:

- **Deposit Protection Service**
- **MyDeposits** — including deposits previously held by Capita
- **Tenancy Deposit Scheme**

Shelter, Citizens Advice and the Land Registry are not deposit schemes.
Scotland and Northern Ireland each run their own separate schemes, so
these three names are the answer only for an England or Wales tenancy.

Source: gov.uk/tenancy-deposit-protection, gov.uk/deposit-protection-schemes-and-landlords

## What a landlord must tell a tenant about a protected deposit, and by when

Within **30 days** of receiving the deposit the landlord must give the
tenant the prescribed information:

- the address of the rented property
- how much deposit was paid
- how the deposit is protected
- the name and contact details of the protection scheme and its dispute
  resolution service
- the landlord's or letting agency's name and contact details
- the name and contact details of any third party that paid the deposit
- why they would keep some or all of the deposit
- how to apply to get the deposit back
- what to do if the tenant cannot get hold of the landlord at the end of
  the tenancy
- what to do if there is a dispute over the deposit

Protecting the deposit and telling the tenant are two separate duties
that share the same 30-day deadline; doing one does not discharge the
other.

Source: gov.uk/tenancy-deposit-protection/information-landlords-must-give-tenants

## Which tenancies the deposit protection rules cover

The England and Wales deposit protection regime applies to **assured
shorthold tenancies that started after 6 April 2007**. A tenancy outside
that description — an older tenancy, a different tenancy type, or one in
Scotland or Northern Ireland — is governed by different rules, and the
30-day and 10-day deadlines above should not be quoted for it.

Source: gov.uk/deposit-protection-schemes-and-landlords

## These figures expire on 1 April 2027

This pack holds the rates in force from 1 April 2026. Because the
minimum wage changes on 1 April every year, from 1 April 2027 the wage
figures here are last year's and should not be quoted as current.

When answering, say which year's rates you are giving. A statutory
figure quoted without its date keeps sounding authoritative long after
it stops being right, and that is the specific way this kind of
information causes harm.

Source: gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates

## State the rate; do not calculate the pay

Everything in this pack is a published value or a published rule. The
questions people ask next — what a week's wages should have been, how
much an underpayment comes to, what a sick employee is owed for eleven
days — are arithmetic on top of these values.

Give the rate and the condition that selects it, then say the
calculation depends on hours, pay reference period and circumstances and
point to gov.uk. A confidently wrong sum about someone's wages is worse
than no sum, because it is actionable.

Source: pack authoring rule, not a gov.uk statement
