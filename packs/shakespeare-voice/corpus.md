# shakespeare-voice corpus

Craft knowledge and short public-domain exemplars. One entry per `## `
heading; each stands alone.

## Modern-to-period vocabulary substitutions

Replace modern words with their Early Modern English forms: you → thou
or thee; your → thy (thine before a vowel); has → hath; does → doth;
before → ere; often → oft; soon → anon; morning → morrow; evening → even
or e'en; why → wherefore; please → prithee or pray thee; between →
'twixt; over → o'er; never → ne'er; it is → 'tis; it was → 'twas.

## The shape of a soliloquy

A soliloquy opens on the dilemma in its first line, often as a question
or an antithesis; the middle turns the problem over through one extended
metaphor; the close resolves into decision or deeper doubt, frequently
sealed with a rhymed couplet. Hamlet's "To be, or not to be — that is
the question" opens with the whole argument compressed into one
antithesis.

## Iambic pentameter in practice

The line is five iambs — ten syllables alternating unstressed and
stressed: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" Variations are
allowed and expressive: an eleventh unstressed syllable (a feminine
ending, as in "To be, or not to be — that is the question"), or an
inverted first foot for emphasis. What is not allowed is prose chopped
into arbitrary line lengths.

## Apostrophe — addressing what cannot answer

The device of turning to an absent person, a thing, or an abstraction as
if it could hear: "O sleep, O gentle sleep, Nature's soft nurse, how
have I frighted thee?" It converts description into drama; prefer it
over reporting a feeling.

## Antithesis — the balanced opposite

Set opposites against each other in one balanced line: "Not that I loved
Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more." The tension between the halves
does the arguing.

## Anaphora — the repeated opening

Repeat the opening words of successive lines or clauses to build
pressure: "This royal throne of kings, this sceptred isle, this earth of
majesty, this seat of Mars." Three repetitions is the working minimum.

## The garden as the state

A sustained period metaphor: the kingdom or the self as a garden —
order requires weeding, neglect breeds "disordered" growth, the gardener
is the governor. Usable for any subject involving governance of a realm
or of oneself.

## The sea and tempest as fortune

Fortune and crisis speak through sea-imagery: "a sea of troubles",
tides in the affairs of men, shipwreck as ruin, harbour as safety. When
a subject involves risk or reversal, the tempest is the period's first
metaphor.

## The stage as the world

"All the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players."
Life as performance — entrances and exits for birth and death, parts and
acts for ages of life. Reach for it when the subject is mortality or the
passage of time.

## Sonnet 18's opening move

"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? / Thou art more lovely and more
temperate." The pattern: propose a conventional comparison as a
question, then immediately exceed it — the beloved is not LIKE the fair
thing, but fairer. A reusable opening for praise.

## Time as devourer

The sonnets' great adversary: Time with a scythe, Time that "decays",
that transfers beauty from face to grave. Praise in this voice often
pivots to defiance of Time — the beloved outlasts it through the verse
itself: "So long lives this, and this gives life to thee."

## Night and its offices

Night belongs to watchfulness, conscience and the supernatural:
"Now is the very witching time of night, when churchyards yawn." Stars
are fate's ministers; the moon is inconstancy; the lark and dawn end
what the nightingale and dark permitted.

## Insult and lament share a grammar

High emotion compresses into stacked epithets and vocatives: "O
villain, villain, smiling, damnèd villain!" for rage; "O, what a noble
mind is here o'erthrown!" for grief. Repetition of the key word, an O of
address, and a final summarizing blow.

## The couplet as a seal

A passage of blank verse may close with two rhymed lines that compress
its argument: "The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience
of the king." Use it to end, never in the middle.
