# einstein-method corpus

His classic thought experiments as worked demonstrations of the method,
one per `## ` heading.

## The train and the lightning — simultaneity is frame-dependent

Two lightning bolts strike the ends of a moving train. An observer on
the embankment, midway between the strikes, receives both flashes
together and calls them simultaneous. A passenger at the train's
midpoint is moving toward one flash and away from the other, so one
arrives first. Neither observer is wrong: simultaneity was defined
operationally (signals meeting at a midpoint), and the definition gives
different verdicts in different frames. Method lesson: an "obvious"
shared fact dissolved the moment it was defined by a measurement
procedure.

## The elevator — equivalence of gravity and acceleration

A person in a closed, windowless elevator cannot distinguish standing on
Earth from being accelerated upward at 9.8 m/s² in empty space; released
objects fall identically in both. From this single imagined box, the
equivalence principle: locally, gravity and acceleration are the same
thing, and light must bend in a gravitational field because it visibly
bends in the accelerating box. Method lesson: one concrete scenario,
taken seriously, forced a new theory of gravity.

## Chasing a light beam — the limit case that broke mechanics

At sixteen Einstein asked: if I ride alongside a light beam at its own
speed, do I see a frozen, motionless wave? Maxwell's equations permit no
such frozen wave, and no experiment ever suggests one. So the limit case
was impossible — the beam recedes at c no matter how fast the chaser
runs — and Galilean velocity addition had to give way. Method lesson:
push a parameter to its extreme and see which cherished assumption
breaks.

## The magnet and the conductor — the asymmetry complaint

Move a magnet near a still coil and theory said an electric field drives
the current; move the coil near a still magnet and theory said a
magnetic force does it — two explanations for what is observably one
phenomenon, differing only in point of view. Special relativity begins,
in its first paragraph, from the offense of that asymmetry. Method
lesson: when descriptions differ but observations do not, the
descriptions carry an error.

## Operational time — clocks, not intuition

"Time" in the method is what synchronized clocks read, and
synchronization is itself a procedure: exchange light signals and adjust
until the round trip halves evenly. Once time is a clock-reading rather
than a universal river, moving clocks may legitimately disagree — and
they do. Method lesson: replacing an intuition with its measurement
procedure is what made the revolutionary step possible.

## The falling painter — the happiest thought

A painter falling from a roof feels no gravity: everything falls with
him, and in his frame gravity has vanished. Einstein called it the
happiest thought of his life, because it located the physics in the
frame rather than in a force. Method lesson: adopt the perspective of
the participant, not only the bystander — some forces exist only from
the outside.

## The EPR box — a thought experiment as objection

To challenge quantum completeness, Einstein imagined two particles
prepared together and separated far apart; measuring one seems to fix
the other's state instantly. No apparatus was built — the imagined
scenario alone sharpened the question so precisely that it became an
experimental program decades later. Method lesson: a thought experiment
can be an argument, rigorous enough to constrain theories on its own.

## Principle first, mechanism later

The method ranks a small number of inviolable principles — the constancy
of c, the equivalence principle, the impossibility of perpetual motion —
above detailed mechanisms, and derives consequences from the principles
alone. Thermodynamics was the model: it knows nothing of molecules yet
is never wrong. When mechanism and principle conflict, the mechanism
loses.
